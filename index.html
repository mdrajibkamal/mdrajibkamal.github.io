Climate Regulation and Bank Market Power: Evidence from a Borrower-Side Shock

with Francisco González and Shams Pathan

This paper studies how environmental regulation affects bank market power through changes in borrower demand. Using the U.S. NOx Budget Program as a regulatory shock, we find that exposed banks experience a decline in market power. The evidence is consistent with a borrower-side channel in which regulation reduces the pool of profitable borrowers and intensifies competition among banks.

Climate Transition Risks and Bank Liquidity Creation: Adapting to Regulatory Shocks

with Francisco González, Steven Ongena, and Shams Pathan

Swiss Finance Institute Research Paper No. 25-75

We examine the causal impact of climate transition risks on bank liquidity creation using the 2004 U.S. NOx Budget Trading Program (NBP), a cap-and-trade regulation implemented in 11 states, as a quasi-natural experiment. Exploiting cross-state and time-series variation, we find that exposed banks reduce liquidity creation. This contraction operates primarily through on-balance sheet adjustments, including lower lending and risky asset holdings, alongside increased off-balance sheet exposures. Banks further respond to earnings pressure by slowing net interest income growth, shifting toward noninterest income, and expanding derivative usage. The effects are stronger among larger, riskier, and weakly capitalized banks, and in markets dominated by large institutions. Short-term institutional ownership amplifies the decline, while higher charter value provides resilience. Results are robust to event-studies, entropy balancing, placebo tests, and spillover controls. Our findings highlight how climate regulations reshape bank intermediation and carry important implications for financial stability.

When Stakeholders Are Not Watching: Corporate Social Responsibility and Biodiversity Risk

with Ahmed Elnahas and Md Noman Hossain

Biodiversity risk offers a rare opportunity to disentangle the win-win, delegated philanthropy, and insider-initiated philanthropy motives of CSR. Using firm-level data on biodiversity risk derived from 10-K filings, we find that firms experiencing higher biodiversity risks increase their CSR engagement. This positive relationship is primarily driven by internal managerial motives, as evidenced by a negative association between CSR and firm value, and by more pronounced effects among entrenched managers and financially unconstrained firms. These results deepen our understanding of how firms respond to emerging environmental risks, such as biodiversity, particularly in contexts of low shareholder awareness and limited external pressure.
